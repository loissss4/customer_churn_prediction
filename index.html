The Problem

Why I Chose Churn

Every subscription business loses customers — the question is whether they see it coming. Replacing a lost customer costs way more than keeping one, so churn quietly drains revenue even when sign-ups look healthy on the surface.

What I set out to do

Build a model that flags customers likely to leave, using their account details, services, contract type, and billing — so a company could actually step in with an offer or a call before that customer walks away, instead of finding out after the fact.

Data & Cleaning

A Dataset That Looked Clean, But Wasn't Quite

What I started with

  • Telco Customer Churn (Kaggle), 7,043 rows, 21 columns
  • Target: Churn (Yes/No)

What I caught

  • TotalCharges was secretly stored as text — 11 rows had a blank space instead of a real value
  • Turned out all 11 were brand-new customers (tenure = 0), so I filled them with 0
  • Otherwise genuinely clean — no duplicates, no messy category spelling
Exploratory Data Analysis

What the Data Was Actually Telling Me

  • Churn is massively concentrated in month-to-month contracts (55% of all customers) — two-year customers barely leave at all
  • Tenure turned out to be the single strongest signal (correlation −0.35) — the longer someone stays, the less likely they are to leave
  • Fiber optic customers churn noticeably more than DSL or no-internet customers — this one surprised me
  • Customers who churned were paying more on average ($79 vs $64 median monthly)
  • No real outliers in tenure, charges — the data was well-behaved here
Feature Engineering

Getting the Data Model-Ready

Encoding & scaling

  • One-hot encoded the multi-category columns (Contract, InternetService, PaymentMethod...)
  • Label-encoded the simple yes/no columns
  • Scaled tenure, MonthlyCharges, and TotalCharges so no single column dominates

Features I created

  • TenureGroup — New / Established / Loyal, to make the pattern human-readable
  • ChargeGroup — Low / Medium / High pricing tier
  • Dropped CustomerID — it's just a label, no predictive signal

The tuned version didn't actually beat the plain default — which taught me that "optimized" doesn't automatically mean "better." I kept the simpler baseline as my final model.

Handling Class Imbalance

Only 1 in 4 Customers Churns — That's a Problem

With churn at just 26.5% of customers, the default model quietly favored the majority class — it looked fine on accuracy but was missing nearly half of the customers who actually left. I retrained with class_weight='balanced' to force it to pay real attention to the minority class.

ModelAccuracyPrecisionRecallF1
Default80.13%65.67%52.67%58.46%
Balanced73.31%49.83%79.14%61.16%

Recall jumped from 53% to 79% — the model now catches 4 out of 5 real churners instead of about half, at the cost of some accuracy and more false alarms. For this business problem, that trade is worth it: missing a leaving customer is usually more expensive than one unnecessary retention offer.

Contract length and tenure were the biggest factors by far — basically, the longer you've committed, the less likely you are to leave. Fiber optic was the clearest warning sign in the other direction.

Recommendations

If I Were Advising the Business

  • Push month-to-month customers toward annual contracts — this is the biggest lever in the whole dataset
  • Focus retention efforts on customers in their first 12 months, since that's where the risk is highest
  • Dig into why fiber optic customers are leaving — my guess is pricing or reliability, but that needs its own investigation
Honest Limitations

What I'd Fix With More Time

Where it falls short

  • Even after balancing, it still misses about 1 in 5 real churners
  • It's one snapshot in time, not a trend — doesn't see a customer's risk building up
  • No complaint logs or competitor pricing data to work with

What I'd try next

  • Class-balancing (SMOTE) to catch more real churners
  • Bring in customer service/complaint history
  • Test Gradient Boosting models (XGBoost/LightGBM)
